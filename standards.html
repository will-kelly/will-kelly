The canon

Editorial standards

This is the canon for everything that ships from this desk: newsletter posts, client deliverables, product copy, and this site. If a draft violates this page, the draft loses.

Why this page is public

Editorial standards only work when someone can hold you to them. Most content operations keep their standards in a drawer, which is how they end up with the appearance of governance instead of governance. Publishing the canon makes it enforceable: readers, clients, and collaborators can point at a specific rule and say a piece broke it.

It also makes the standards reusable. If you run a content operation and don't have a canon, take this one and adapt it. Attribution is nice but not required.

Voice canon

  • Active voice. Always. Teams create documentation debt by deferring governance. Documentation debt is not "created by teams."
  • Lead with the claim. The argument appears in the first paragraph, ideally the first sentence. No warm-up, no scene-setting, no background.
  • Name the dysfunction before the fix. Pattern recognition comes first: connect the specific symptom to the systemic cause, then propose the remedy.
  • Diagnose structures, not individuals. The structural cause gets named. People don't get blamed for organizational patterns.
  • Write to the person who owns the budget and the risk. The default audience is CIO, CTO, and CISO decision-makers and the senior practitioners who advise them.
  • Zero vendor theater. If a claim reads like a press release, cut it or counter it. Performative adoption gets named, not celebrated.
  • Specific beats general. "Teams lose 30% of institutional knowledge when a senior writer leaves" beats "knowledge management is important."
  • One argument per piece. Not one per section. One for the whole piece.
  • Skepticism is the baseline. Enthusiasm has to be earned by evidence.

Match the register to the channel

Everything under this byline uses one of two registers. Register determines structure, opening, paragraph rhythm, and close. The registers never blend in a single piece.

Register Where it runs How it opens How it closes
Publication voice
Utility-first and structured. Every technical claim connects to cost, risk, or compliance.		 Trade press, white papers, executive briefs, client deliverables Acknowledges a real tension or gap, then pivots to a practical framework A concrete call to action, never a summary
Editorial voice
Confrontational where enterprise dysfunction deserves it. Short paragraphs. The reader is implicated, not just informed.		 Substack, LinkedIn, hot commentary The indictment, with one sentence of context maximum A line that leaves a mark, never a summary

Subheadings do work in both registers. They function as action instructions, not topic labels. "Build your governance framework before you select a vendor" earns its place. "AI governance frameworks" doesn't.

Build every argument from four parts

  1. Claim. A specific, arguable assertion. Not "AI is complicated" but "most enterprise AI rollouts fail at the workflow integration layer, not the model selection layer."
  2. Evidence. A data point, case, or observed pattern that supports the claim. Named and cited where possible, described as a pattern where not.
  3. Stakes. Why it matters to the reader: cost, risk, competitive consequence, or career exposure.
  4. Call. What the reader should do, think, or stop doing. Every piece closes with a position.

If any of the four is missing, the draft goes back.

Cut the banned list on sight

Every entry below represents a pattern corrected in production more than once. No exceptions, no justifications accepted.

Banned What to do instead
"Leverage" as a verbUse "use," "apply," "run," or "deploy"
"Utilize"Use "use"
"In today's rapidly evolving landscape"Delete the opener and start on the argument
"It's worth noting"Delete the preamble. If it's worth noting, note it.
"Seamlessly"Name what actually happens, friction included
"Game-changer"Specify what changes and for whom
"Best-in-class," "world-class"Cite the benchmark or cut the claim
"It could be argued that"Make the argument or don't
"Many experts believe"Name the experts or cite the research
Passive voiceFlip it. The actor takes the front of the sentence.
Rhetorical questionsConvert to declarative claims. Questions weaken authority.
Exclamation pointsNever. Not in body copy, callouts, or product listings.
Summary conclusionsEnd with the implication or the call to action
Any sentence that could appear verbatim in an article about a different technologyRewrite until it couldn't

Mechanics that hold across both registers: contractions preferred, Oxford comma always, sentence case in titles and headings, numerals for 10 and above and for every percentage. Short sentences land harder than long ones. When in doubt, break the sentence.

Source claims or drop them

  • No factual claim ships without a confirmable source. If a real source can't be confirmed, the claim gets cut or rephrased as practitioner observation.
  • Statistics get attributed to named sources: analyst firms, standards bodies, foundations, and academic institutions first; vendor research only with explicit vendor attribution.
  • Never anonymous blogs, unattributed statistics, or vendor marketing copy.
  • Links come from existing body text, not bolted-on "further reading" sections. Every link target gets verified as real and current at publication. A plausible-looking URL that can't be confirmed doesn't ship.

Run the checklist before anything ships

Every draft, including AI-assisted drafts, clears this checklist before delivery. The pattern recognition and practitioner credibility can't be generated, only checked and applied.

  • Banned phrases: scanned and clean
  • Passive voice: under 8% in publication register, under 5% in editorial register
  • Opening: starts with tension, gap, or indictment, not background
  • Subheadings: action instructions, not topic labels
  • Argument: claim, evidence, stakes, and call all present
  • Close: call to action or diagnostic implication, not a summary
  • Claims: every statistic attributed to a named source
  • Links: every URL verified as real and current
  • Register: matched to the channel

What does not ship

  • Vendor-endorsed "here's why this platform is great" pieces, unless commissioned and labeled sponsored.
  • Generic best-practices listicles without a diagnostic angle.
  • Any piece that treats vendor adoption as inherently good.
  • Any closer resembling "as AI continues to evolve, the organizations that adapt will thrive."
  • Drafts that haven't cleared a voice check.

The standards grow when the same correction happens twice. If you catch this site breaking its own canon, say so.
